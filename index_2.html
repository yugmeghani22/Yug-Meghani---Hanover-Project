Question 02 · From the call

Intercompany expense allocation, understood.

From Chris · On the call
The expense allocations for intercompany payables. The concept of paying from the management company if you're a private equity fund, then reimbursing back to the funds, and then allocating the expenses to the different funds by a certain allocation rule dependent on the invoice.
My understanding · Custom, not AI

How the flow actually works.

Two entities, side by side

In a private equity firm, there are two distinct entities running side by side — the management company and the funds. The difference matters: the management company is the operating business, employing the staff, paying rent, signing vendor contracts. The funds, on the other hand, are separate legal vehicles that hold the LPs' committed capital. They're financially independent from the firm itself, even though the firm runs them.

The round-trip, in plain terms

So when a vendor sends an invoice — let's say $100K from outside legal counsel — the management company will normally pay it first out of its operating cash, the same way it would pay rent or any other expense. Operationally, this is just easier: one entity, one wire, one vendor relationship. But legally, that payment can't sit on the management company's books indefinitely. The work was done for one of the funds, so the cost has to find its way back to the fund that actually benefited.

That round-trip — mgmt co paying first, then redistributing — is what creates the "intercompany" relationship: the mgmt co becomes a creditor of the funds, and the funds owe reimbursements back.

Why it's tracked this carefully

The reason it's tracked this carefully comes down to fund accounting first principles. Every transaction has to be traceable back to the partner level, so it can be allocated correctly according to ownership and what each LP actually agreed to pay for in the LPA. If a cost lands on the wrong fund — or worse, sits with the LPs when it should have stayed with the GP — that becomes both an accounting problem and a fiduciary one.

The allocation rule This makes more sense after reading the articles — still a lot to learn and conceptualize

The allocation rule is the math that determines how much each fund owes for any given expense. It's not arbitrary — it's set by the LPA (the contract between the GP and the LPs), and it varies based on the type of expense. The most common rules:

  • Pro-rata by committed capital — most common for general firm-wide expenses (audit fees, fund admin, shared legal).
  • Pro-rata by NAV or invested capital — used for investment-related costs, where the basis should reflect what's actually deployed.
  • Direct attribution — when an expense is clearly for one specific fund (e.g., legal work on Deal XXI). No pro-rata math needed; the cost belongs entirely to that fund.
  • Portion stays with the mgmt co — for expenses that can't be passed to the funds at all, like partner salaries or GP overhead. These come out of the management fee, not the LPs' pockets.

The methodology gets formalized in a document agreed with the auditors, and it's the reference point everything traces back to. The math is rule-based, but the rules change — methodologies get updated mid-life of a fund (new vehicle launches, side letters, auditor turnover), and someone has to make sure the calculation engine reflects the latest version. That's where most of the real errors live.

Why this matters for the treasury seat

Intercompany payables are one of the six payment types I mapped in Question 01 — they're ~4 of the 25 daily payments Hanover Park processes. They're also where methodology version drift is the highest-frequency near-miss class.

The treasury seat sits in the middle of the flow: reviewing the AI-prepped allocation, confirming the methodology is current, catching mixed-attribution edge cases before the wire goes. This is one of the highest-leverage areas to bring discipline to — because the math is rule-based, but the rules change, and someone has to notice when they do.
